# Academia

The likeliest way this project fails is not that we find nothing. It is that we find something, write it up, and discover it was named and solved in a law review in 1990. Constitutions have been read adversarially by serious people for two centuries, and the specific question this project asks — whether a document can be used lawfully to destroy the order it establishes — has its own literature, its own vocabulary, and its own unsettled arguments. This folder is that literature.

The reason to keep it at the center rather than in an appendix is that the existing theory tells us where to point the search. A constitution is a large object and most of it is procedural detail that no one could weaponize. The scholarship has already isolated the parts that fail: emergency clauses, amendment rules, term limits, delegation, the machinery for replacing a constitution wholesale. We would find some of those on our own, slowly, and we would miss others entirely.

## What goes in

An entry earns its place by naming a mechanism. Work arguing that democracies are fragile, or that constitutions embody values, does not go here no matter how well argued, because it does not tell us where to look. Work arguing that a specific clause type fails in a specific way does, and the more precisely it names the clause the more valuable it is. Where a paper identifies the provision by number in an actual constitution, that number is recorded here, because that is the level at which this project operates.

Negative results are kept and marked as such. If the formalization literature has never handled a document at constitutional scale, that fact saves us more time than a dozen papers that almost did.

Live disagreements are preserved rather than resolved. Where the scholarship splits on whether the Enabling Act of March 1933 satisfied the Weimar amendment procedure, both sides are recorded, because the split is the interesting part and a confident summary of it would be a fabrication.

## Searches are conducted in the relevant language

This rule was written after breaking it. Two independent searches reported that no comparative literature on enabling acts existed, and both were wrong: the Austrian Academy of Sciences published a twenty-article open-access volume on exactly that subject in 2018, covering seven countries. It is in German. Both searches were in English, and both returned a confident negative that one page of a table of contents refutes.

A negative result from a search is a fact about the search. Before this folder records that something is missing, the search is repeated in the language of the jurisdiction — German for Austria and Weimar, Italian for the Statuto, and so on — and the claim of absence is qualified by the languages actually searched. Where a gap is real but only in English, that is stated as a translation and circulation problem rather than a research one, because those call for different work.

## What does not go in

No PDFs. This repository is public, most of this work is under copyright, and the entries are pointers rather than reproductions. Each carries enough of a citation to find the work in any library and a link where a legitimate free copy exists. Summaries in these files are written here, not quoted from the source.

## How entries are recorded

Every entry carries a full citation, an access marking, and a few sentences on what it gives this project specifically. Access is one of free, paywalled, book, dataset, software, or blocked, and blocked means we confirmed the barrier rather than assumed it.

Every link in this folder was retrieved and confirmed to serve the work it claims to serve. A citation that could not be verified is either omitted or marked as unverified in the entry itself. This is the same rule the corpus runs under, for the same reason: a plausible citation that does not resolve is worse than no citation, because it looks like evidence.
