# Interwar constitutional collapse

Gödel's answer to the examiner was about Austria. Not about theory, not about Article V — about a specific country whose constitution had been turned into a dictatorship within his adult lifetime, in a city where he was living at the time. Whatever he found in the American document, he found it while holding a worked example.

This file collects the scholarship on the three collapses that were available to him, and it is organized around a single question: in each case, was the constitution *used* or *broken*? The answer is different in all three, and the differences are the useful part. Germany fell through an amendment procedure that imposed only procedural limits. Austria fell through an enabling act inherited from the empire that the constitution never mentioned. Italy fell through ordinary legislation, with its constitution formally intact and unamended from beginning to end.

Only one of those three is a loophole in the sense this project means. That is worth sitting with before assuming the search is well posed.

## The theory Gödel could have been reading

**Clinton Rossiter, "Constitutional Dictatorship: Crisis Government in the Modern Democracies"** (Princeton, 1948). Controlled digital lending at https://archive.org/details/constitutionaldi0000ross

Published the year after the hearing, and the closest contemporaneous statement of this project's question. Rossiter's thesis is that democracies build temporary dictatorship into their own constitutions — emergency powers meant to be used and then surrendered — and that the mechanism designed to save the state is the mechanism by which it is lost. He works from the Roman dictatorship forward through Article 48, the French state of siege, and American war powers, and he names the provisions. There is no evidence Gödel read it. It is what the informed argument looked like at that moment.

**Frederick M. Watkins, "The Failure of Constitutional Emergency Powers under the German Republic"** (Harvard, 1939). https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.278406

Earlier, narrower, and written while the outcome was still unfolding. The first systematic argument that Article 48 was not incidental to the republic's destruction but the instrument of it.

**Karl Loewenstein, "Militant Democracy and Fundamental Rights," I and II,** 31 American Political Science Review 417 and 638 (1937). Paywalled at JSTOR (https://www.jstor.org/stable/1948164); a third-party scan of Part I circulates at https://dagobah.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Loewensetin-1937-Militant-Democracy.pdf

Loewenstein names the vulnerability precisely: rights of speech, assembly, and party formation can be used by movements that intend to end them, and a constitution that protects those rights unconditionally protects its own destruction. This is the direct ancestor of the German Basic Law's eternity clause and its party-ban provision — which makes it the first serious attempt to *patch* the class of defect this project is looking for.

**Ernst Fraenkel, "The Dual State"** (Oxford, 1941), https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.13142, and **Franz Neumann, "Behemoth"** (Oxford, 1942; 2nd ed. 1944), https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.10130

Two émigré lawyers, two incompatible accounts of what the Nazi state actually was, both free. Fraenkel says it was a dual state — a normative half that kept applying law predictably in commercial and civil matters, and a prerogative half that could override any norm for political reasons, with the coexistence being the point. Neumann says it was not a state at all but competing power centers with no unifying legal order, and that the Enabling Act destroyed the legal system rather than amending it. The disagreement matters here: on Fraenkel's account the legal order survived and was hollowed, on Neumann's it was annihilated and the legality was theater. Those imply different things about whether a formal analysis of the text would have caught anything.

**Carl Schmitt, "Legality and Legitimacy"** (1932; Duke, 2004, trans. Seitzer). Book, paywalled.

Schmitt made this project's argument in 1932, before it happened, about Article 76: an amendment procedure that is neutral as to content — that will ratify whatever a two-thirds majority approves — cannot protect the order it belongs to, because it cannot distinguish an amendment from an abolition. He was right, he published it a year before the Enabling Act, and he then joined the party that proved him right. The uncomfortable fact for this project is that the clearest interwar statement of the loophole thesis comes from the jurist who used it.

**Schmitt v. Kelsen on the guardian of the constitution** (1931), collected in Lars Vinx, ed., "The Guardian of the Constitution" (Cambridge, 2015): https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/guardian-of-the-constitution/A978F4C7C2FD6C4B559C1C439BBE338C

The Vienna debate, and the one Gödel had the best chance of having absorbed by osmosis. Schmitt: the guardian must be the President, acting under Article 48. Kelsen — who wrote the Austrian constitution and built the first constitutional court — the guardian must be a court. Each identifies a real failure mode in the other's answer, and the interwar record went on to vindicate both critiques. **David Dyzenhaus, "Legality and Legitimacy: Carl Schmitt, Hans Kelsen, and Hermann Heller in Weimar"** (Clarendon, 1997), https://academic.oup.com/book/2553, is the best English route in, and recovers Heller, who had the most defensible position and died in exile in 1933 at forty-two.

## Germany: the amendment procedure

The mechanism was Article 76, which required a two-thirds quorum and a two-thirds majority of those present, and imposed **no limit on what an amendment could contain**. The Enabling Act was drafted as a constitutional amendment and invoked Article 76 explicitly. Passed by the Reichstag on March 23, 1933, dated and in force March 24.

**The German Bundestag's own account is free and better than most secondary summaries:** https://www.bundestag.de/resource/blob/189778/enabling_act.pdf

Two things in it are worth extracting, because the popular story gets both wrong.

First, the arithmetic. The usual telling is that Göring rigged the count by excluding the 81 Communist deputies, and that this is what made the Act pass. The Bundestag states the opposite in its own words: with 444 in favor and 94 against, "there would have been a sufficient majority for a constitutional amendment even if the Communist deputies had been able to take part in the vote." The exclusion was insurance, not the load-bearing element. Strip the procedural irregularity away and the Act still passes.

Second, the conclusion the Bundestag draws is not that it was lawful. It is that "even today it remains a moot point whether the adoption of the Enabling Act was actually consistent with the provisions of the Weimar Constitution." Ninety years on, with no shortage of motive to settle it, the German parliament's public position is that the question is open.

That combination — clean numbers, unresolved legality — is the single most instructive fact in this file. It says the defect was not in the vote count. It was in a document that told you how to amend it and never told you what could not be amended.

**Peter Caldwell, "Popular Sovereignty and the Crisis of German Constitutional Law"** (Duke, 1997), https://muse.jhu.edu/book/70696/, is the source that names this directly: the Weimar-era scholarly consensus held that Article 76 imposed procedural limits only, never substantive ones. Caldwell traces how that consensus made the Enabling Act *thinkable* as law rather than as a coup. If any single work in this bibliography describes the Gödelian defect in a real constitution that really fell, it is this one.

**Marc de Wilde, "The state of emergency in the Weimar Republic,"** 78 Legal History Review 135 (2010). Abstract free at https://dare.uva.nl/search?identifier=dcada9f2-1dab-44d9-8ff4-13e352cffcf1; full text paywalled at Brill.

Recovers the 1924 Jena conference where Schmitt and Erwin Jacobi argued that Article 48 let the President derogate from the constitution's rule-of-law provisions in order to preserve its "political substance." This is the moment Article 48 was re-theorized from a safety valve into an instrument of transformation — nine years before it was used that way.

**Christoph Gusy, "Die Weimarer Reichsverfassung"** (Mohr Siebeck, 1997), German, book. The standard legal treatment. Gusy's diagnosis of Article 48 is that it limited the *duration* of emergency decrees but never their *substance*, and that Ebert's frequent early use — well over a hundred invocations — normalized the instrument long before anyone abused it. **Christopher Adair-Toteff, "Dictatorial Power and States of Exception in the Weimar Republic"** (Routledge, 2025) is the most recent full treatment in English.

**Hans Mommsen, "The Rise and Fall of Weimar Democracy"** (UNC, 1996). Book. The chapters "The Road to Presidential Dictatorship" and "Government by the Threat of Coup d'État" are the corrective to the seizure narrative: Hitler was appointed under the ordinary appointment power, and the Act passed by vote. The republic was handed over by constitutional actors using constitutional procedures.

**The Preussenschlag, July 20, 1932,** is the rehearsal. Papen used Article 48 to depose the elected Prussian government; the Staatsgerichtshof ruled in October, split the difference, affirmed the Article 48 basis in principle, and changed nothing, because the power had already moved. Henning Grund, *"Preußenschlag" und Staatsgerichtshof im Jahre 1932* (Nomos, 1976), German. Hagen Schulze's article title asks the question in two words — "Preußenschlag oder Staatsstreich?", *Vierteljahrshefte für Zeitgeschichte*, https://www.jstor.org/stable/43641144, paywalled — and the two words are exactly the distinction this project needs to be able to draw.

## Austria: the borrowed enabling act

Austria did not fall through its constitution. It fell through a statute the constitution inherited and never mentioned, and this is the most important structural fact in this file.

The instrument is the **Kriegswirtschaftliches Ermächtigungsgesetz, RGBl. 307/1917** — an imperial wartime economic enabling act, signed at Reichenau on July 24, 1917 by Karl, the last emperor. Full German text, free: https://www.verfassungen.at/at-18/kriegsermaechtigung17.htm (the site refuses requests without a browser user-agent string; it is live). Its § 1 authorizes the government to issue by ordinance whatever measures are necessary to promote and restore economic life, avert economic harm, and supply the population with food and other necessities, for the duration of the extraordinary circumstances caused by the war. Its § 3 is the check: ordinances must be laid before the Reichsrat, quarterly while it sits, and withdrawn on its demand. Its § 6 charges "the participating ministers" with execution.

It should have died with the empire. It did not, because **§ 7(2) of the Übergangsgesetz, StGBl. 451/1920**, carried it forward — and carried it forward, in the provision's own words, to the federal government *and to the individual federal ministers*. A 1917 wartime decree power, held by single ministers, inside a 1920 democratic republic. It was not repealed for Austria until a constitutional act of July 25, 1946, BGBl. 143/1946 — meaning it was formally on the books until the year before Gödel's hearing.

The editorial note on that same page states the endpoint plainly: in 1934 the **Bundes-Verfassungsgesetz 1934 was itself issued on the basis of this law**. A constitution promulgated under the authority of an imperial economic decree statute. If this project ever needs one sentence to justify its own existence, that is the sentence.

Then it got worse in a way no amount of reading the constitution would have revealed. In their 1922 commentary — **Kelsen, Fröhlich & Merkl, "Die Bundesverfassung vom 1. Oktober 1920"** (Deuticke, 1922), German, book — the authors concluded that the 1917 law's parliamentary oversight provision did *not* survive the transfer, because the transitional law did not name the Nationalrat as the Reichsrat's successor for that purpose. The check was read out of the instrument by the constitution's own architect, eleven years before the government began issuing KWEG ordinances in October 1932 on the road to the corporate state.

The full chain has a gazette citation at every link: RGBl. 307/1917 → StGBl. 451/1920 § 7(2) → the 1922 commentary → Dollfuß's ordinances from 1932. **Not one link is in the constitution.** A complete formal analysis of the 1920 B-VG, done perfectly, finds nothing.

**The parliamentary self-elimination of March 4, 1933.** Haus der Geschichte Österreich, free, German: https://hdgoe.at/zerstoerung-parlamentarismus During a contested vote all three presidents of the Nationalrat resigned in succession, and the rules of procedure had no provision for that situation — nobody was left with authority to reconvene the chamber. Dollfuß called it a self-dissolution and then prevented reconvening, by force where necessary. hdgö's verdict: *der juristische Staatsstreich war vollzogen*.

This is a pure gap. Not an abused power, not a stretched clause — an unhandled case in the procedural rules, exploited by resigning into it.

**The neutralization of the Constitutional Court,** May 1933. hdgö, free: https://hdgoe.at/ausschaltung_vfgh The sequence is a quorum attack. Parliament-nominated members resigned on May 18, 20, and 22; the government then decreed on May 23 that parliament-nominated members could sit only if *all* of them were present, which after the resignations was impossible; further resignations pushed the court below quorum. hdgö calls it unconstitutional outright. Kelsen's court, disabled through its own composition rules.

**The 1929 Novelle** introduced direct popular election of the President, presidential powers to dissolve the Nationalrat and appoint and dismiss the government, and — in Article 18 — an emergency ordinance power. hdgö: https://hdgoe.at/bundes-verfassungsnovelle-1929

**Ewald Wiederin, "Das Notverordnungsrecht des Bundespräsidenten,"** BRGÖ 8/2 (2018) 385–395, DOI 10.1553/BRGOE2018-2s385. Open access, verified: https://verlag.oeaw.ac.at/api/download/content/9783700183860_kapitel_385_395.pdf

Wiederin's English abstract states three things this project can rely on. The 1920 constitution concentrated all power in parliament and **consciously renounced any state-of-emergency provision** — the omission was deliberate, not an oversight. The 1929 amendment introduced the presidential emergency decree power, but the Social Democrats extracted a condition in negotiation: such decrees require the approval of a standing parliamentary subcommittee. And **the power has never been used, to this day**. Dollfuß ignored it and reached for the 1917 law instead.

That first point independently confirms what our own corpus file shows — Article 18 of the 1920 B-VG has exactly two paragraphs and no emergency power. Two sources, arrived at separately.

The lesson is uncomfortable for formal methods. The constitution's emergency power was well designed and never touched, and its quality was irrelevant, because the attacker used a different instrument.

**Emmerich Tálos, "Das austrofaschistische Herrschaftssystem: Österreich 1933–1938"** (LIT, 2013), German, book, is the standard modern account and documents which KWEG ordinances exceeded the 1917 law's economic scope. **Alexander Balthasar,** "The Austrian path to the constitution of 1 May 1934 — An application of the paradigm of 'Militant Democracy' just *avant la lettre*!?", 61 Hungarian Journal of Legal Studies 159 (2020), record at https://real.mtak.hu/134381/, is in English and argues the contested reading that Dollfuß was defending Austria against Nazism. Most scholars disagree. It is worth reading anyway, because it lays out the claimed constitutional basis for each step, which is what this project needs regardless of how the claim is judged.

## Italy: no loophole required

Italy is the control case, and it is the reason this file exists in this form.

The Statuto Albertino of 1848 was *flexible* — it contained no special amendment procedure and no entrenchment, so ordinary legislation could change anything. Mussolini therefore had no need of a loophole, an emergency clause, or an enabling act. He used statutes. The Acerbo Law of November 18, 1923 awarded two-thirds of the seats to any list taking more than a quarter of the vote. Law 2263 of December 24, 1925 made the head of government answerable to the King alone and stripped Parliament of the power to remove him. The *leggi fascistissime* of 1925–26 abolished press freedom, banned opposition parties, and created the Special Tribunal. Every one an ordinary act. **The Statuto was never amended, and remained formally in force through the entire fascist period.**

**Antonio La Spina, "La 'flessibilità' dello Statuto albertino e il regime fascista"** (2021), open access, Italian: https://apcz.umk.pl/TSP-W/article/view/38221/ makes the distinction explicit — flexibility versus rigidity — and concludes that Italy differs from Germany and Austria in kind: nothing had to be violated or stretched, because nothing was entrenched.

**Adrian Lyttelton, "The Seizure of Power: Fascism in Italy, 1919–1929"** (1973; Routledge, 2004), book, adds the part that matters for motive: the legal façade was maintained *deliberately*, because the regime needed constitutional continuity to keep the monarchy, the bureaucracy, and conservative elites on board. Legality was not a constraint the fascists worked around. It was an asset they chose to preserve. **Alberto Aquarone, "L'organizzazione dello Stato totalitario"** (Einaudi, 1965), Italian, remains the standard documentary study.

The consequence for this project is direct. A constitution with no entrenchment has no Gödelian defect to find, and falls anyway. Formal contradiction is not necessary for lawful collapse, and searching for it in a flexible constitution is a category error. Whatever we build has to know the difference before it starts.

## What this changes

Three collapses, three mechanisms, one shared feature: in each case the actors went to considerable trouble to stay inside the forms. That is the empirical warrant for taking Gödel seriously at all. It is not warrant for the specific claim that the defect lives in the amendment clause — one of these three fell that way.

The Austrian and Italian cases both suggest the search space is larger than the constitutional text. Austria's fatal instrument was a statute the constitution inherited silently; Italy's were ordinary laws a flexible constitution never restricted. If this project analyzes only constitutional documents, it would have missed both, and it would have missed the one Gödel was actually talking about.

## Gaps

I nearly published a bad one here, and the correction is more useful than the claim would have been. The draft of this file asserted that no dedicated treatment of the Austrian enabling-act chain existed, on the strength of two independent searches that both reported the same absence. One page of an Austrian Academy of Sciences table of contents refutes it. The literature exists, it is open access, and it is comprehensive — it is simply in German. **Absence from an English-language search is not absence.** Both searches were English-shaped, and both returned a confident negative.

What survives as a real gap is narrower and worth stating precisely. There is little **English-language** scholarship on the Übergangsgesetz § 7(2) mechanism, so the case is effectively unavailable to the comparative constitutional-design literature that would most benefit from it — which is a translation and circulation problem, not a research one.

**No article makes the Enabling Act's Article 76 validity its central question.** The debate runs through monographs — Mommsen, Evans, Caldwell — and Gusy's 2018 contribution treats enabling legislation functionally rather than adjudicating that specific question. For something the Bundestag publicly calls unresolved, the absence of a dedicated treatment is still notable, though it should be checked against the German literature before being asserted.

The corresponding claim in the abusive-constitutionalism file — that comparative scholarship does not treat enabling acts as a category of constitutional design — has been corrected there for the same reason.
