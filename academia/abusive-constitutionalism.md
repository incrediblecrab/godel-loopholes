# Abusive constitutionalism

Gödel's claim was that a constitution could be used, lawfully, to destroy the order it establishes. He had no name for that and neither did anyone else in 1947. The field does now. Between roughly 2012 and 2020 comparative constitutional law converged on the problem from several directions at once, and the result is a body of work that has already catalogued most of the moves: which clauses fail, in what order, and what the failure looks like from inside while it happens.

The closest named category is Nancy Bermeo's executive aggrandizement — an elected leader who weakens legislatures, courts, and accountability bodies through legal, incremental steps, each defensible on its own. That is Gödel's scenario with the mysticism removed, and Bermeo's finding is that it is one of the three forms of democratic breakdown that have become *more* common since the Cold War, while the coup and the stolen election have become rarer. The route Gödel worried about is the route that won.

What follows is organized by what each work gives us rather than by prestige. Access markings were confirmed by retrieval; where a publisher blocks access and an author deposit does not, both are recorded.

## The framework

**David Landau, "Abusive Constitutionalism,"** 47 U.C. Davis Law Review 189 (2013). Free: https://lawreview.law.ucdavis.edu/sites/g/files/dgvnsk15026/files/media/documents/47-1_Landau.pdf

The foundational paper, and the one to read first. Landau separates two routes — amendment and wholesale replacement — and argues that replacement is the more dangerous, because a new constituent assembly reconfigures everything at once and resets whatever constraints the old text imposed. His clause-level targets are amendment procedures that clear on a single parliamentary supermajority, judicial appointment mechanisms, and constituent-assembly provisions. He also argues that the standard defenses, militant democracy and the unconstitutional-amendment doctrine, mostly fail against a determined actor who simply avoids triggering them.

**Nancy Bermeo, "On Democratic Backsliding,"** 27 Journal of Democracy 5 (2016). Paywalled; abstract and supplementary coded data free: https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/on-democratic-backsliding/

The taxonomy. Six forms of backsliding, three rising and three declining. The rising three are promissory coups, executive aggrandizement, and strategic electoral manipulation; the declining three are open-ended coups, executive coups, and election-day fraud. Bermeo's observation that contemporary backsliding is legitimated by the very institutions democracy promotion prioritizes is the sentence that most directly restates Gödel's problem.

**Aziz Huq & Tom Ginsburg, "How to Lose a Constitutional Democracy,"** 65 UCLA Law Review 78 (2018). Free at the publisher: https://www.uclalawreview.org/lose-constitutional-democracy/

Distinguishes authoritarian reversion, which is sudden and near-total, from constitutional retrogression, which is gradual. Their argument is that the United States Constitution was built against the first and is weak against the second, and they name three pillars retrogression attacks: competitive elections, political speech and association, and the rule of law including judicial independence. They rate United States protection weak on all three.

**Kim Lane Scheppele, "Autocratic Legalism,"** 85 University of Chicago Law Review 545 (2018). Free: https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclrev/vol85/iss2/2/

The script, in five steps: win an election, use the mandate to rewrite constitutional rules, disable courts and accountability bodies legally, entrench loyalists in terms long enough to outlast successor governments, then keep holding elections that cannot change anything. Her Hungary work is the most granular mapping available from constitutional text to autocratic outcome, and the companion pieces are worth having: Bánkuti, Halmai & Scheppele, "Hungary's Illiberal Turn: Disabling the Constitution," 23 Journal of Democracy 138 (2012), paywalled, and "Understanding Hungary's Constitutional Revolution" (2015), free at https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/understanding_hungarys_constitutional_revolution.pdf

The Hungarian vulnerability she identifies is worth stating precisely, because it is a clause-level finding of exactly the kind this project hunts: amendment required a two-thirds parliamentary majority and nothing else. No referendum, no second body, no delay. One election delivering a supermajority was sufficient to reach every part of the constitutional order.

**Daniel Hemel, "Self-Coup and the Constitution,"** 37 Constitutional Commentary 315 (2022). Free: https://scholarship.law.umn.edu/const-comment/vol37/iss3/2/

The most directly Gödel-adjacent modern paper. Hemel treats the autogolpe as a category of constitutional design rather than of political history, and asks what in a constitution's structure resists it. He separates a Hamiltonian defense, which relies on elite control of executive selection, from a Madisonian one, which relies on separation of powers, and tests both against Fujimori in 1992 and against January 6.

**Mark Tushnet, "Constitutional Hardball,"** 37 John Marshall Law Review 523 (2004). Free: https://repository.law.uic.edu/lawreview/vol37/iss2/7/

Names the class of moves that are legal but violate settled understandings of how the system is played. Tushnet's condition for hardball appearing is that actors perceive a chance at permanent transformation of the constitutional order, which is the same trigger the backsliding literature identifies. Joseph Fishkin & David Pozen, "Asymmetric Constitutional Hardball," 118 Columbia Law Review 915 (2018), free at https://scholarship.law.columbia.edu/faculty_scholarship/2452/, adds that hardball is not evenly distributed between parties and that the asymmetry tracks whether a party's constitutional narrative frames norm-breaking as recovery.

**Ozan Varol, "Stealth Authoritarianism,"** 100 Iowa Law Review 1673 (2015). Free: https://ilr.law.uiowa.edu/sites/ilr.law.uiowa.edu/files/2023-02/ILR-100-4-Varol.pdf

The most direct challenge to this project's premise, and it should be read as such. Varol's argument is that the modern autocrat works below the constitutional level entirely — through electoral rules, broadcast licensing, NGO registration requirements, and anti-terrorism and anti-corruption statutes used as pretexts. If he is right, a project that reads only constitutional text will miss most of what matters. Austria supports him: the instrument Dollfuß used was a 1917 wartime statute, not a constitutional clause.

## The mechanisms

**Mila Versteeg, Timothy Horley, Anne Meng, Mauricio Guim & Marilyn Guirguis, "The Law and Politics of Presidential Term Limit Evasion,"** 120 Columbia Law Review 173 (2020). Free: https://www.annemeng.com/uploads/5/6/6/6/56666335/term_limits.pdf

The empirical study, covering 234 incumbents across 106 countries since 2000. A third of leaders reaching the end of a prescribed term attempt evasion. The methods, in rough order of frequency, are formal amendment, judicial reinterpretation, constitutional replacement, installing a controlled successor, and delaying elections under emergency pretext. Two findings matter especially here: courts usually validate evasion rather than block it, and popular protest is the more effective check.

The paper also records a named structural loophole in the American text. Akhil Amar's argument is that a president could serve sixteen years by alternating between the presidency and the vice presidency, since the Twenty-second Amendment limits election to the presidency while the Twelfth bars from the vice presidency only those ineligible to the presidency, and the Twenty-second makes a two-term president ineligible for *election* rather than ineligible to *hold* the office. Whether or not it works, it is the right shape: a fixed point produced by two clauses that each behave correctly alone. This is the closest thing in the literature to a worked example of the object Gödel described, and it deserves its own treatment.

At Gödel's 1947 vantage none of this applied, because there was no term limit at all. The two-term tradition was Washington's convention and nothing more, and Franklin Roosevelt had just broken it four times over. Any reconstruction of Gödel's argument has to be built against a Constitution with no limit on presidential tenure whatsoever.

**Rosalind Dixon & David Landau, "Constitutional End Games: Making Presidential Term Limits Stick,"** 71 Hastings Law Journal 359 (2020). Free: https://repository.uclawsf.edu/hastings_law_journal/vol71/iss2/4/

Argues counterintuitively that permanent reelection bans may be worse than consecutive-term bans, because a permanent bar creates an end game in which the incumbent's only route to a future is extraordinary measures. A design finding that cuts against intuition, and a reminder that a clause can fail by being too strict.

**John Ferejohn & Pasquale Pasquino, "The Law of the Exception: A Typology of Emergency Powers,"** 2 International Journal of Constitutional Law 210 (2004). Publisher blocked; free via author deposit: https://www.researchgate.net/profile/John-Ferejohn/publication/30946990_The_Law_of_the_Exception_A_Typology_of_Emergency_Powers/links/58fc9c180f7e9ba3ba5576ad/The-Law-of-the-Exception-A-Typology-of-Emergency-Powers.pdf

Separates the Roman dictator model, in which concentrated authority is granted to one person for a fixed term, from the legislative supremacy model, in which emergency is handled by adapting ordinary law. Their three dangerous features are executive-initiated declaration without legislative approval, absent sunset provisions, and no judicial review of the declaration itself. Austria's 1920 text is a useful control here: it had no emergency ordinance power at all until the 1929 Novelle added one to Article 18.

**David Kosař & Katarína Šipulová, "Comparative Court-Packing,"** 21 International Journal of Constitutional Law 80 (2023). Publisher blocked; free via author deposit: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369900053_Comparative_court-packing/fulltext/64320ccc20f25554da1b52d0/Comparative-court-packing.pdf

Three techniques rather than one: expansion, emptying through forced retirement or abolition, and swapping. Their clause-level correlates are whether court size is fixed in the constitution or set by statute, whether tenure is life or fixed-term, whether appointment runs through one actor or several, and what it takes to create or abolish a court. A constitution that leaves court size to ordinary legislation has a hole in it, and the United States is such a constitution.

**David Landau, "Constraining Constitutional Change,"** 50 Wake Forest Law Review 859 (2015). Free: https://wakeforestlawreview.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/50WakeForestLRev859.pdf

Why rigid amendment rules underperform. A supermajority requirement constrains only the amendment route, and an actor who cannot clear it calls a constituent assembly or a referendum instead. Rigidity displaces pressure rather than absorbing it.

## Design and defense

**Karl Loewenstein, "Militant Democracy and Fundamental Rights,"** 31 American Political Science Review 417 and 638 (1937), in two parts. Free: https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/history/students/modules/hi290/seminars/revolution/lowenstein_militant_democracy_i.pdf and https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/history/students/modules/hi290/seminars/revolution/lowenstein_militant_democracy_ii.pdf

Written in 1937, watching it happen. Loewenstein states the paradox plainly: a constitution that protects association and party formation has thereby armed anyone who wants to campaign for its abolition, and he coins the legal revolutionary for the actor who does. His proposed defenses — party bans, restrictions on speech advocating overthrow, loyalty requirements — became Articles 21 and 79(3) of the German Basic Law. This is the direct ancestor of every eternity clause now in force, and it was written by an emigre from the same collapse Gödel watched.

**Yaniv Roznai, "Unconstitutional Constitutional Amendments: The Limits of Amendment Powers"** (Oxford University Press, 2017). The book is not freely available, but the LSE doctoral thesis it revises is: https://constitutionalist.com.ua/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Roznai_Unconstitutional-constitutional-amendments.pdf

The theoretical treatment of unamendability, explicit and implicit. Roznai's working distinction is between constituent power, which makes a constitution, and amendment power, which is itself constituted and therefore limited — the amendment clause cannot reach everything, because it is a creature of the thing it would destroy. That is a self-reference argument in legal dress and it is the natural bridge to the formal side of this project. His observation about the American text is worth carrying: Article V's only unamendable provision protects equal state suffrage in the Senate, and nothing in the document is unamendable on grounds of democracy as such.

**Tom Ginsburg & Alberto Simpser, eds., "Constitutions in Authoritarian Regimes"** (Cambridge University Press, 2014). Book.

Argues that constitutions in dictatorships are not decorative. The uncomfortable corollary for this project is that democratic-looking features — elections, rights catalogues, courts — can stabilize autocratic rule rather than constrain it, which means a clause cannot be scored in isolation from who controls its interpretation.

**Rosalind Dixon & David Landau, "Abusive Constitutional Borrowing"** (Oxford University Press, 2021). Book.

How democratic constitutional concepts get borrowed and inverted. Same warning as Ginsburg and Simpser, argued through Hungary, Poland, Venezuela, Cambodia, Rwanda, and Fiji.

## Where the literature disagrees

These splits are live, and they are more useful to us than the points of agreement, because each one marks a place where the answer is not yet known.

Whether replacement or amendment is the greater danger is unsettled. Landau ranks replacement first; Dixon and Landau later show amendment and judicial reinterpretation doing the same work; Varol argues the real action is below the constitutional level entirely, which if true limits what any textual analysis can find.

Whether militant democracy works is unsettled. Loewenstein proposed it and the German Basic Law implements it, but Landau argues that party bans and eternity clauses are either hard to deploy against abusive constitutionalism or simply avoidable by an actor who declines to trigger them.

Whether courts help is unsettled and the evidence is not encouraging. Versteeg and colleagues find courts usually validate term-limit evasion. Kosař and Šipulová go further and build a framework for when court-packing is legitimate as democratic repair, which sits awkwardly against Scheppele and against Huq and Ginsburg, who treat packing as an autocratic instrument.

Whether erosion reverses is unsettled. Bermeo reads contemporary backsliding as more reversible than its predecessors; Scheppele reads autocratic legalism as self-reinforcing through long-term appointments and redrawn districts that survive a change of government.

## What is missing

There is no comparative literature on enabling acts **in English**. That qualifier is load-bearing, and it was added after the fact. The claim originally stood unqualified here, on the strength of a direct search that returned a clean negative — and it is wrong. *Beiträge zur Rechtsgeschichte Österreichs* 8/2 (2018) is a twenty-article open-access volume doing exactly this, comparatively, across Austria, Germany, Italy, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, and Brazil. It is catalogued in the interwar-collapse file. It is in German, and two independent English-language searches did not see it.

What is true is narrower: in the anglophone comparative-constitutional-design literature, delegation of legislative power to the executive is not treated as a category the way court-packing and term limits are. The German Enabling Act appears constantly as an illustration and almost never as an object of study. The topic is dispersed into the emergency-powers and executive-aggrandizement literatures and loses its shape there.

That dispersal sits directly on top of this project's evidence. Austria fell through a 1917 wartime enabling statute carried into the republic by a transitional provision, and Germany fell through an enabling act passed under a contested reading of its own amendment clause. Two of the three interwar cases turn on an instrument the English-language design literature does not name — while a German-language literature has been studying it for years.

The methodological lesson is worth more than the finding. A negative result from a search is a fact about the search, and every search in this repository so far has been English-shaped.
