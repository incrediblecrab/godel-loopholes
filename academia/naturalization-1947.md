# Naturalization, 1947

The statute Gödel was examined under is evidence about the claim he made, and it has not been treated that way. Every account of the hearing turns on Morgenstern's memory of what was said in the room, and none of them asks what the examiner was legally required to establish, what the applicable bar to naturalization actually prohibited, or what the Supreme Court had said four years earlier about the very article the reconstructions converge on. The answers change the picture, and one of them is a primary source that says a good deal of what Gödel is supposed to have discovered.

## The governing statute

The Nationality Act of 1940, Public Law 76-853, 54 Stat. 1137, effective January 13, 1941, governed the hearing of December 5, 1947. The amendments passed between 1945 and 1947 dealt with quotas and with racial eligibility for military personnel and their spouses and did not touch the civilian requirements.

Its residence, character and attachment requirements were carried into the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 without substantive change and are now at 8 U.S.C. § 1427(a), which requires that the applicant "has been and still is a person of good moral character, attached to the principles of the Constitution of the United States, and well disposed to the good order and happiness of the United States." Free, verified: https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1427&num=0&edition=prelim

The attachment formula has been in American naturalization law since 1802. The modern gloss, which states the test rather than changing it, is that attachment "implies a depth of conviction which would lead to active support of the Constitution" and excludes applicants "hostile to the basic form of government of the United States." Free, verified: https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-12-part-d-chapter-7

## The bar Gödel was arguably standing on

Section 313 of the 1940 Act, now 8 U.S.C. § 1424, barred from naturalization any person who advocates "the establishment in the United States of a totalitarian dictatorship," and any person who advocates the overthrow of the government "by force or violence or other unconstitutional means."

The wording is worth reading slowly, because both limbs miss what Gödel was doing. The first bars advocacy of establishing a dictatorship, and he was not advocating one. The second bars advocacy of overthrow by unconstitutional means, and his entire claim was that the means were constitutional. A man arguing that the Constitution lawfully permits its own destruction falls outside a prohibition drafted against people who want to destroy it unlawfully, which is a gap in the statute of exactly the kind this project catalogues, sitting in the statute governing the hearing at which the observation was made.

Whether an examiner would have drawn that distinction under pressure is a different question, and Morgenstern's account suggests everyone present understood the risk. Nothing in the memo indicates the distinction was ever articulated.

**This section's statutory text is not yet verified against the primary source.** The wording above is reported from secondary compilations and from the current codification, and the 1940 text at 54 Stat. 1137 has not been read. The Library of Congress `tile.loc.gov` service that supplied this project's Statutes at Large images is organised by Congress rather than by volume, its identifiers run `llsl-c1` through at least `llsl-c54`, which is volume 29, and `llsl-c76` returns 404. The LOC collection search API returns 403 to scripted requests. Until 54 Stat. 1137 is in hand this entry is marked unverified on the statutory wording and reliable only on the substance.

## Schneiderman v. United States, 320 U.S. 118 (1943)

This is the find, and it was not in any account of the hearing this project has seen.

Schneiderman is a naturalization case. It asks whether a man who advocated radical constitutional change could be "attached to the principles of the Constitution," under the same statutory standard Gödel faced four years later. To answer it the Court had to say what Article V permits, and it did.

Free, verified from the official page images: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep320/usrep320118/usrep320118.pdf

At 320 U.S. 137, in the opinion of the Court, Justice Murphy wrote:

> Article V contains procedural provisions for constitutional change by amendment without any present limitation whatsoever except that no State may be deprived of equal representation in the Senate without its consent. Cf. *National Prohibition Cases*, 253 U. S. 350. This provision and the many important and far-reaching changes made in the Constitution since 1787 refute the idea that attachment to any particular provision or provisions is essential, or that one who advocates radical changes is necessarily not attached to the Constitution.

That is the United States Reports, four years before the hearing, stating that the amendment article carries no limitation whatever beyond the Senate proviso. The word *present* is doing work, since the Article I limitations on the slave trade and on direct taxation had expired by their own terms in 1808, and with those gone the Court is describing an amendment power with a single surviving substantive restriction.

The second passage matters as much, and it is the reason the first cannot be reported as a holding. At 320 U.S. 140:

> It was argued at the bar that since Article V contains no limitations, a person can be attached to the Constitution no matter how extensive the changes are that he desires, so long as he seeks to achieve his ends within the framework of Article V. But we need not consider the validity of this extreme position for if the Government's construction is accepted, it has not carried its burden of proof even under its own test.

So the Court described Article V as substantively unlimited, called the strong inference from that an extreme position, and expressly declined to rule on it. Both passages were read from the page images rather than from the optical character recognition layer.

The combination is the useful thing. The proposition that Article V has no substantive limit was in the U.S. Reports in 1943. The proposition that this implies anything one likes may lawfully be done through Article V was flagged by the Court as open and left open. A reconstruction of Gödel's argument that consists only of "Article V has no substantive limits" therefore reconstructs something the Supreme Court had already printed, and the part that would be novel is precisely the part the Court refused to decide.

`what-counts-as-a-finding.md` requires a candidate to establish that the literature has not already named it. For any Article V candidate this project raises, Schneiderman is now the first citation that has to be answered.

A footnote at 320 U.S. 140 adds context worth keeping: in 1938 Congress failed to pass a bill barring naturalization to any person believing in "any form of government for the United States contrary to that now existing in the United States." The broad ideological bar was considered and not enacted, which is part of why the enacted bar is drafted as narrowly as it is.

The *National Prohibition Cases*, 253 U.S. 350 (1920), cited in the passage above, is the authority for the Article V proposition, and it has since been retrieved and read from the Library of Congress page images. Free, verified: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep253/usrep253350/usrep253350.pdf It matters here for a reason separate from Schneiderman: it fixed the base of Article V's two-thirds requirement as a quorum rather than the entire membership, and counsel in that case had already computed the minimum-coalition figure this project treats as one of its own procedures. Both points are worked out in `analysis/united-states-1947/threshold-arithmetic.md`.

The route that worked is worth recording, because three others failed. Justia returns a Cloudflare 403, CourtListener's cluster endpoint now requires authentication and its HTML returns an empty 202, and Wikisource has no page for the case. The Library of Congress `usrep` path is reliable and takes the form `usrep<volume>/usrep<volume><first page>/usrep<volume><first page>.pdf`.

## The examination in practice

There was no standardised question list. The Immigration and Naturalization Service's own historical account states that courts administered the tests "orally, extemporaneously, and with little uniformity across jurisdictions," that examiners retained discretion over which questions to ask, and that during the 1930s the Service worked to eliminate trick questions and to ensure that questioning established attachment to constitutional principles rather than memorised facts. Free, verified: https://www.uscis.gov/about-us/our-history/stories-from-the-archives/origins-of-the-naturalization-civics-test

This matters for reading the memo. The examiner's question about the Austrian government was not a departure from a script, because there was no script, and a question about the applicant's country of origin and its form of government is squarely the kind of question the attachment inquiry invited. The coincidence in the story is smaller than it looks.

The process ran in two stages, a preliminary examination by a Service examiner and a final hearing in a naturalization court where the judge had final authority and could ask his own questions. Witnesses had to be citizens who had known the applicant for the full five-year period and attested to character and attachment, which is what Einstein and Morgenstern were doing in the room.

The judge was Phillip Forman, of the District of New Jersey, appointed by Hoover in 1932 and elevated to the Third Circuit in 1959. He had administered Einstein's oath in 1940. Free, verified: https://www.historynjdc.org/judges/judicial-biographies/phillip-forman

## The Morgenstern memorandum

Free, verified, full text: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Godel_naturalisation/

The memorandum was dictated by Oskar Morgenstern on September 13, 1971, roughly twenty-four years after the hearing, and opens by stating that it is written "on the basis of memory only without consultation of my notes and diaries." His widow gave a copy to the Institute for Advanced Study on August 30, 2005; Dawson, writing in 1997, had been unable to locate it.

The reliability assessment is straightforward and should be stated wherever the memo is used. It is the only first-person account by a participant, it is internally consistent, and it matches the independently checkable facts. It is also a reconstruction of dialogue from memory after twenty-four years, by a man who says so in his first sentence, and it gives the year as 1946 when the hearing was December 5, 1947. A source that is wrong about the year is not thereby wrong about the substance, but the quoted exchanges are not a transcript and should never be cited as one.

The Institute's own catalogue entry for the item is blocked to scripted requests, confirmed at 403: https://albert.ias.edu/entities/archivalmaterial/9fd45e83-9706-4c1f-92de-302efdc85561

## What Gödel actually studied, and what survives

The memo is the only account of his preparation, and it describes him working from the settlement of North America forward through American history to constitutional law, then to Princeton's borough and township government, and then to the Constitution, where he found what he called inner contradictions.

His philosophical notebooks are the obvious place to look for the argument, and the relevant one is *Max Phil XIV*, covering July 1946 to May 1955. It is digitised and downloadable. Free, verified, 6.0 MB: https://www.ias.edu/sites/default/files/library/Max14%201946%201955%20pt1.pdf The Institute's landing page for the papers is blocked at 403, so the file must be fetched directly.

A page was rendered to test the real obstacle and it is exactly as described. The notebook is written in Gabelsberger shorthand, a nineteenth-century German stenographic system, at high density, with only scattered Latin-script words legible between the shorthand. Reading it is a specialist skill and not something this project can do incidentally. The critical edition in progress is *Philosophische Notizbücher*, De Gruyter, for the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences. Book. An orienting scholarly account is Crocco and Engelen, "Kurt Gödel's Philosophical Remarks (Max Phil)". Free, verified at the versioned URL, since the unversioned one returns 500: https://hal.science/hal-01473665v1/document

No transcription of the 1947 pages has been published, and no separately catalogued constitutional notes by Gödel have been identified in any finding aid.

## Gaps

The naturalization file itself has apparently never been sought. The petition, the examiner's recommendation and any record of the final hearing for the District of New Jersey would sit in Record Group 21 at the National Archives facility in New York City, undigitised. No published scholar appears to have requested it. It may contain nothing beyond a form, and it is still the only unexamined contemporaneous record of the event, as against a memory written down in 1971.

The statutory text of the 1940 Act at 54 Stat. 1137 is not in hand, for the retrieval reasons recorded above.

*Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Kansas*, 248 U.S. 276 (1919), has since been retrieved and read at the LOC path `usrep248/usrep248276/usrep248276.pdf`, and the finding is recorded in `analysis/united-states-1947/threshold-arithmetic.md`. What remains open is a close reading of its full opinion rather than its syllabus and argument pages.

Whether Gödel's argument appears anywhere in *Max Phil XIV* is unknown, and answering it requires Gabelsberger. That is a bounded, concrete, unattempted task with a real chance of a null result, which makes it a better use of effort than another reconstruction from the same three sentences of Morgenstern.
