# Article V entrenchment

Guerra-Pujol's reconstruction of Gödel's loophole is a two-step move: amend Article V to strip the clause protecting equal state suffrage in the Senate, then use the weakened procedure. Zahoransky and Benzmüller formalize that move. Both are recent. The question they turn on is not, and this folder exists because `method/what-counts-as-a-finding.md` names "already in the literature and uncited" as a disqualifier.

It was a live question in American law reviews for at least the four decades before Gödel sat the exam, it was genuinely unsettled, and no court had decided it. One pre-1947 Supreme Court case, Barry (1929), bears on the clause's scope and is recorded below; none bears on whether the clause can be amended away. This entry records that literature, restricted to work published before December 5, 1947, because work published after that date cannot bear on what Gödel could have been reasoning from.

Two questions run through all of it. **Q1: does the proviso protect only the arithmetic of equal representation, or the Senate's continued existence?** **Q2: can the proviso be repealed by ordinary Article V amendment and the protection then removed — the two-step?**

Every quotation below was read from the scanned text layer of the source named, not from a summary. Page numbers were confirmed against the running heads on surrounding pages.

## The broad reading: the proviso protects the Senate itself

**Arthur W. Machen, Jr., "Is the Fifteenth Amendment Void?", 23 Harvard Law Review 169 (1910).** Free: https://archive.org/details/jstor-1324228

The earliest direct statement of the position, at pages 172-173, and the one closest to what the two-step has to defeat. Machen holds that the proviso "necessarily requires the continuance of the Senate as an integral part of the federal legislature," and that a state "could not be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate merely by abolishing the Senate, or reducing it to a body merely advisory, concentrating all legislative power in the House of Representatives." He runs the same argument one step further: since a state must exist to enjoy equal suffrage, the proviso entails that no state can be abolished either.

If Machen is right, the Senate cannot be hollowed out without the entrenchment clause being violated, and Gödel's step one becomes load-bearing rather than optional. This is the strongest published argument against the narrow reading and it predates every modern treatment.

**William L. Marbury, "The Limitations Upon the Amending Power," 33 Harvard Law Review 223 (1919).** Free: https://archive.org/details/jstor-1327162

The same conclusion by a different route, at page 228: the prohibition cannot be "nullified, indirectly, by taking away from the state any of those functions which are essential 'to its separate and independent existence' as a state." Marbury reaches indirect nullification specifically, which is nearer to the two-step than Machen gets.

**Frederick L. Siddons, "Constitutional Aspects of the Tillman-McLaurin Controversy," 12 Yale Law Journal 21 (1902).** Free: https://openyls.law.yale.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/9e7f9f6a-71f6-4248-a756-532acf4ede65/content

The earliest of the four and the most aggressive, though its subject is Senate discipline rather than amendment. After the Senate temporarily disabled both South Carolina senators for brawling on the floor, Siddons argued at page 23 that "the effect of suspending Senators Tillman and McLaurin was, for the time being at least, to deprive South Carolina of its right of equal suffrage in the Senate," and at page 25 that the suspension was therefore unconstitutional.

He is careful, and the care matters. He does not say senators cannot be expelled. At page 24 he concedes that "if his offense was of such a character as would justify his expulsion, this punishment may be inflicted," and asks only for procedure: the state should be notified and given "an opportunity given to select and appoint his successor," failing which "it may well be held to have waived its right of equal suffrage, and the judgment of expulsion should be made effective." So even the broadest pre-1947 reading of the clause stops short of immunity from expulsion. No pre-1947 authority asserting that immunity was found.

Siddons is here rather than under Q1 because his premise is the functional one — that the clause protects an actual capacity to vote, not a formal seat count — and because the Supreme Court took the opposite view twenty-seven years later. See *Barry* below.

## The narrow reading: zero senators each is still equality

**Lester B. Orfield, *The Amending of the Federal Constitution* (University of Michigan Press and Callaghan & Co., 1942), Michigan Legal Studies.** Book. Free scan of the first edition: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.505449

The most thorough pre-1947 treatment and the one that most directly contradicts the broad reading. At page 97 Orfield argues from the drafting history that the clause is confined to equality: Sherman moved a proviso protecting both state existence and Senate equality, his motion failed, and Gouverneur Morris's equality-only clause was adopted immediately after. At page 99 he calls the broad reading "legal casuistry" — "the implied limits deduced from the equality clause are hung on too small a peg."

At page 98 he states the argument this project has to take seriously: "Similarly, if the Senate were abolished, the equality of suffrage would not be disturbed, as each state would have no senators at all."

**That sentence deserves attention on its own terms.** Orfield offers it as a reductio against Machen, not as a recommendation. But read as it stands it describes a lawful path that never touches the entrenchment clause: abolish the Senate by ordinary amendment, and the proviso is satisfied vacuously because no state has been given fewer senators than any other. If that reading is right, Gödel's step one is not merely inert in a formal model — it is unnecessary in law, because the clause it removes never blocked the destination. This is recorded here as an observation about the literature, not as a candidate path; it has not been worked through the standard in `method/what-counts-as-a-finding.md`, and Orfield's own framing is against it.

On Q2 Orfield is careful, and his caution cuts against the two-step. At page 85 he notes the suggestion that the limitation "might be disregarded directly, or if not directly, indirectly by first repealing the clause and then depriving a state of its equal representation" — the two-step, stated in 1942 — and he does not endorse it. He records that the clause could certainly be repealed by an amendment ratified by *all* the states, and that the same result follows if the affected states plus enough others to reach three-fourths adopt it. Those are the uncontroversial cases, and neither is a loophole, because the states whose suffrage is at stake have consented.

At pages 157-158 he gives the temperature of the field: "There are not many who assert that the ordinary amending body could abolish the equal suffrage clause. It would seem that under the prevailing legalistic interpretation of the Constitution an absolute prohibition against amendment could be overcome only by revolution."

That is the single most useful sentence in the pre-1947 record for this project, and it is unfavourable. The two-step, in 1942, was a minority position that a leading treatise declined to adopt.

**W. F. Dodd, "Amending the Federal Constitution," 30 Yale Law Journal 321 (1921).** Free: https://archive.org/details/jstor-787395

Leans narrow. At page 330 Dodd acknowledges Machen and calls the extension to state existence "an even more remote limitation," grouping these arguments with "strained applications of this language, by remote implications."

## What no one had settled

**H. Rottschaefer, *Constitutional Law* (1939), 9-10.** Book, not independently obtained; quoted in Orfield at pages 85-86 and recorded here at second hand for that reason.

Rottschaefer states that no case had been found "in which the power to amend has been employed to directly or indirectly modify a constitutional provision expressly excepted from that power," and that the issues such an attempt would raise "could not be settled by any reasoning derived by logical processes from prevailing conceptions of sovereignty." He inclines toward holding such attempts futile, while conceding there is nothing to cite.

**Dodge v. Woolsey, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 331 (1855), at 348.** Free in any reporter.

Dictum calling the clause one of the "permanent and unalterable exceptions to the power of amendment." It decides nothing, and Orfield treats it as an error at pages 84-85, since even that clause can be repealed with unanimous consent.

**Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), at 615-616.** Official U.S. Reports scan, read from the page images: https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep279/usrep279597/usrep279597.pdf

This is the closest thing to a pre-1947 holding on the *scope* of the clause, and it reads it narrowly. The Senate had refused to seat Vare pending an investigation, and Pennsylvania argued this deprived it of equal Senate suffrage. Sutherland, for the Court:

> The equal representation clause is found in Article V, which authorizes and regulates amendments to the Constitution, "provided, . . . that no state, without its consent, shall be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate." This constitutes a limitation upon the power of amendment and has nothing to do with a situation such as the one here presented. The temporary deprivation of equal representation which results from the refusal of the Senate to seat a member pending inquiry as to his election or qualifications is the necessary consequence of the exercise of a constitutional power, and no more deprives the state of its "equal suffrage" in the constitutional sense than would a vote of the Senate vacating the seat of a sitting member or a vote of expulsion.

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions for present purposes.

The first needs stating carefully, because two overstatements are available and both are wrong. *Barry* does not contradict Machen or Marbury, and it does not endorse Orfield. What it decides is narrow: a temporary failure to seat a senator, arising from the Senate's Article I power to judge elections and qualifications, is not an Article V deprivation of equal suffrage. The Court's own phrase is "temporary deprivation of equal representation" — so it is not even a case where the formal two-each arithmetic held. Pennsylvania did not have two participating senators. The Court distinguished constitutional entitlement from temporary occupancy, and Article V amendment from Article I proceedings.

What that squarely rejects is Siddons's 1902 application of Article V to Senate discipline, which is the only pre-1947 attempt found to give the proviso work outside the amending power. It leaves the question dividing Machen from Orfield — whether an *amendment* abolishing or hollowing out the Senate deprives states of equal suffrage — entirely open. A reader in 1930 could consistently hold that *Barry* was right about seating, that Machen was right about permanent abolition by amendment, and that Marbury was right about indirect structural nullification. An earlier version of this file said *Barry* rejected the "shared premise" of the functional reading; that was overreach and is withdrawn.

The second is blunter. The closing comparison is explicit that a vote of expulsion does not deprive a state of equal suffrage "in the constitutional sense." That is dictum and it concerns one seat rather than both, but it is the Supreme Court saying, before the vantage date, that the entrenchment clause is not an obstacle to expulsion. Any argument that Article V blocks a reduction of the Senate by expulsion has to get past this sentence.

So the pre-1947 record splits into three questions, not two, and only the first has an answer.

**Q0, domain.** Does Article V constrain temporary vacancies produced by Article I proceedings? *Barry*: no.

**Q1, structural scope.** Does an amendment abolishing or hollowing out the Senate deprive states of equal suffrage? Undecided. Machen and Marbury say yes; Orfield says no.

**Q2, self-entrenchment.** Can the proviso itself be repealed and the protection then removed? Undecided, and this is the one the two-step needs.

**Q2 had no judicial answer before December 5, 1947.** That is the finding, and it holds regardless of which way the scholarship leaned. Gödel's claim was that a lawful path existed; the pre-1947 record shows the decisive question was open, which is consistent with his claim without being evidence for it.

## Unverified

**Blewett Lee, "Abolishing the Senate by Amendment," 16 Virginia Law Review 364 (1930).** Paywalled: https://www.jstor.org/stable/1065399

Existence confirmed — the JSTOR record resolves, and Orfield cites it at exactly this volume and page in footnote 36 on page 98, attached to the zero-senators sentence. The full text could not be obtained: Virginia Law Review volume 16 postdates the cutoff for JSTOR's free Early Journal Content, and HathiTrust holds it search-only.

**Its argument is therefore unverified and is not relied on anywhere in this repository.** Orfield's placement of the footnote suggests Lee states the narrow, zero-senators position. Web summaries assert the opposite, that Lee argued the Senate cannot be abolished without unanimous consent. Those summaries are machine-generated and were not traced to the text. The title alone does not settle it, since a paper called "Abolishing the Senate by Amendment" could argue either way. Obtaining this article through a library is worth doing, because on the narrow reading it is the closest published antecedent to the observation recorded above.

**Skinner, "Intrinsic Limitations on the Power of Constitutional Amendment," 18 Michigan Law Review 213 (1920)** — free at https://archive.org/details/jstor-1277271 — was read and is *not* on point. It argues implied limitations from the Ninth and Tenth Amendments rather than from the equal suffrage proviso. It is listed here so that the next person does not spend the time.

## What this changes

The two-step is not new and this project must stop treating it as though the modern literature invented it. Orfield states it in 1942 and declines it, and the position that the ordinary amending body could reach the clause was, on his account, held by few.

Machen and Marbury on one side, Orfield and Dodd on the other, no case either way: the disagreement is preserved here rather than resolved, per this folder's rule, because the split is the substance. Any candidate path that runs through Article V entrenchment has to say which side it is standing on and answer the other.

The searches behind this entry were conducted in English only, which is adequate here because the subject is American constitutional scholarship published in American law reviews.
