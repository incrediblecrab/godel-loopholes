# The Enabling Act path

## Texts and recensions

Four steps, four documents, all in `corpus/germany-1933/`.

The Weimar constitution is cited from `08-11-1919-rgbl-1383-reichsverfassung-wikisource-transcription.txt`, the gazette recension at RGBl. 1919 p. 1383. The English in `08-11-1919-reichsverfassung-mcbain-rogers-1922-english.txt` is used for nothing load-bearing, because the two decisive observations below are about individual German words and a translation cannot carry them.

The decree is cited from `02-28-1933-rgbl-i-83-reichstagsbrandverordnung-wikisource-transcription.txt`, RGBl. I 1933 p. 83. The Enabling Act from `03-24-1933-rgbl-i-141-ermaechtigungsgesetz-wikisource-transcription.txt`, RGBl. I 1933 p. 141.

## The path

**Step 1. Article 48(2) is invoked to suspend the seven basic rights, without a stated end.**

Article 48(2) permits the Reichspräsident, where public security and order are seriously disturbed or endangered, to take the necessary measures, and for that purpose to set aside *vorübergehend*, temporarily, in whole or in part, the rights fixed in Articles 114, 115, 117, 118, 123, 124 and 153.

Section 1 of the decree sets aside those same seven articles *bis auf weiteres*, until further notice.

Those two adverbs are not synonyms. *Vorübergehend* asserts that the condition will end. *Bis auf weiteres* asserts only that it continues until someone decides otherwise, and the someone is the party that issued it. The authorizing clause requires a suspension bounded in time; the exercise is bounded by the will of the person exercising it.

The reason this is a finding rather than a quibble about German usage is internal to the document. Section 2 of the same decree, two paragraphs later, uses *vorübergehend* — the Reich government may *vorübergehend* assume the powers of a Land's highest authority. The drafter had the constitution's own word available, used it correctly in the very next section, and did not use it in the section where the constitution required it. The substitution is demonstrable from the document's own vocabulary without reference to anything outside it.

Legal status: contested, and this is the strongest textual objection available against the decree. It was not the objection that was made at the time.

**Step 2. The decree removes the conditions under which the Reichstag could be constituted in opposition.**

The rights set aside by § 1 include Article 114, personal liberty, Article 118, freedom of expression including the press, and Article 123 and Article 124, assembly and association. The decree then states that restrictions on those rights, searches, seizures and interference with the post are permissible beyond the limits otherwise fixed by law.

Nothing in this step is a defect in the decree. It is what suspending those articles means. The step is in the path because of what it does to Step 3.

Legal status: settled, on the assumption that Step 1 was valid.

**Step 3. Article 48(3), the check, remains in force and does not fire.**

Article 48(3) requires the President to inform the Reichstag of every measure taken under paragraphs 1 or 2 without delay, and provides that the measures shall be set aside on the Reichstag's demand.

This is a real check. It is unconditional, it needs no supermajority, and it was never amended or repealed. It did not fire, and the reason is not in its own text: it requires a Reichstag willing and able to demand revocation, and Step 2 removed the conditions under which such a Reichstag could be assembled.

This is the disableable-operator mode in `method/attack-surfaces.md`, and it is the reason the sixth column of the power inventory exists. A table that records "the Reichstag may revoke" has recorded a check. A table that also asks what happens if the holder of the power is hostile to the Reichstag has recorded that the check is downstream of the power it constrains.

Legal status: settled that the check existed. Not contested that it was formally available. It was simply never exercised.

**Step 4. The Enabling Act is passed under Article 76 and switches Article 76 off.**

Article 76 permits amendment by ordinary legislation, subject to two-thirds of the statutory membership being present and at least two thirds of those present assenting, and imposes no limit whatever on content. The Act's preamble states that the requirements of constitution-amending legislation were satisfied, which is the invocation.

Article 3 of the Act then provides that Articles 68 to 77 of the constitution do not apply to laws decided by the government. Article 76 is inside that range. The amendment procedure was used to pass a law that removed the amendment procedure from the government's legislative path.

This is the cycle, and it is what the reference-graph enumeration in `method/enumerations.md` is for. It is visible from the two texts alone, with no historical knowledge, as a provision that reaches itself.

Legal status: contested to this day. The German Bundestag's own published account, cited in `academia/interwar-collapse.md`, states that whether the Act's adoption was consistent with the Weimar constitution remains a moot point. Ninety years and considerable motive have not settled it.

## Minimum coalition

Article 76 composes two fractions. Two thirds of the statutory membership must be present, and two thirds of those present must assent. Two thirds of two thirds is four ninths, so the minimum assent that satisfies the article is about forty-four percent of the statutory membership.

A rule described everywhere as a two-thirds requirement can be met by less than half the chamber. That is the whole of the arithmetic and it needs no source beyond the clause.

The actual vote was not close to the minimum. `academia/interwar-collapse.md` records the Bundestag's figures, 444 for and 94 against, and the Bundestag's own conclusion that the majority would have sufficed for a constitutional amendment even had the eighty-one Communist deputies been able to take part. The exclusion of those deputies was insurance. It was not load-bearing, and an analysis that rests on it is resting on the wrong thing.

## The limits that held and did not matter

Article 2 of the Act carried substantive limits. Laws decided by the government could deviate from the constitution only insofar as they did not have as their object the institution of the Reichstag and the Reichsrat as such, and the rights of the Reichspräsident were to remain unaffected.

Both limits were observed. The two bodies continued to exist and the presidency was left alone while Hindenburg lived. The state was reorganized regardless, because neither limit protected anything that had to be removed.

Article 5 carried two sunsets, and the second is the more instructive. The Act was to lapse on April 1, 1937, and to lapse further if the present government were replaced by another. The second condition makes the power's continuation depend on the holder not being displaced, which gives the holder of the power a written incentive to prevent its own displacement, using the power. A termination condition that can only be triggered by an event the power itself exists to prevent is not a limit. It is a statement of what the holder must avoid.

## Falsifier

The claim of this folder is that the four steps above are each traceable to a cited clause, that the only one requiring a contested reading is Step 1, and that Step 4 is a genuine self-reference rather than a loose description of one.

It would be wrong if Article 3 of the Act did not in fact include Article 76 in the disapplied range, which is checkable in one line of the corpus file; if *bis auf weiteres* and *vorübergehend* were interchangeable in German legal usage of the period, which would need a contemporaneous source and would defeat Step 1 while leaving Step 4 standing; or if a demand under Article 48(3) was in fact made and rejected, which would move Step 3 from an unfired check to a broken one and would change what the case demonstrates.

## The edit that closes it

Article 76 needed a sentence saying what could not be amended, and the absence of that sentence is the defect. This is not hindsight applied to a document that could not have known better: the same absence is what `academia/interwar-collapse.md` records the Weimar-era scholarly consensus as having affirmed, that Article 76 imposed procedural limits and never substantive ones.

The Act's own Article 2 shows the drafting was available. It is a substantive limit on what a law may have as its object, written in 1933 by the beneficiaries, in one sentence. The constitution could have carried the same sentence about itself and did not.

What the case does not license is the conclusion that a substantive limit would have been sufficient. Austria's enabling instrument carried three limits, none of which was ever repealed, and a whole constitution was promulgated under it anyway. Limits are necessary and demonstrably not sufficient, and any candidate elsewhere in this project that proposes a limit as its closing edit has to say why that limit would be enforced when these were not.
