# Probing the presidential Inability silence

`silence-inventory.md` row 11 is the only entry in the twelve-row table with no filler at all. Article II, Section 1 provides that on the President's "Inability to discharge the Powers and Duties of the said Office, the Same shall devolve on the Vice President," and as of December 5, 1947 nothing whatsoever settled what "Inability" means, who decides it exists, or how it ends. This is a targeted probe of that silence, scoped to four narrow questions.

## The constitutional text

From `corpus/united-states-1947/09-17-1787-constitution-parchment-nara-transcription.txt`, Article II, Section 1:

> In Case of the Removal of the President from Office, or of his Death, Resignation, or Inability to discharge the Powers and Duties of the said Office, the Same shall devolve on the Vice President, and the Congress may by Law provide for the Case of Removal, Death, Resignation or Inability, both of the President and Vice President, declaring what Officer shall then act as President, and such Officer shall act accordingly, until the Disability be removed, or a President shall be elected.

Note the clause's own vocabulary: it says "Inability" twice and then "Disability" once. The clause itself does not say whether these are the same condition, though Congress treated them as interchangeable when it legislated under this very clause in 1947, which is set out below. The clause authorizes Congress to legislate the line of succession past the Vice President but does not authorize Congress to define "Inability" as it applies to the President alone. It does not say who determines that the condition exists. It does not say whether "the Same shall devolve" means the office permanently passes (the Tyler reading) or the powers are temporarily exercised (the acting-president reading).

## What the pre-1947 record established, and what it did not

### Tyler, 1841: succession settled, inability untouched

When William Henry Harrison died on April 4, 1841, Vice President John Tyler insisted he had become the President, not merely an acting one. He took the presidential oath, moved into the White House, and refused to open mail addressed to "Acting President." Congress acquiesced: a joint resolution referred to him as "the President." The Tyler precedent settled succession for death, and by extension for resignation and removal.

It did not settle inability, and worse, it created a trap for inability. If "the Same shall devolve" means the vice president becomes president permanently — as Tyler established for death — then invoking the clause for a president who might recover would permanently strip the recovering president of the office. A vice president who acted on inability under the Tyler reading would become president outright and could not be displaced when the original president recovered. This made everyone unwilling to invoke the clause for anything short of death.

The Tyler precedent is drawn from secondary historical sources and has **not** been verified against primary documents (the 1841 Congressional Globe or Harrison administration papers) to this project's page-image standard. The facts stated — Tyler's oath, his insistence on the title, Congress's acquiescence by joint resolution — are uncontested in every secondary source consulted, but the project's evidentiary rule requires the caveat.

### Garfield, 1881: eighty days of paralysis

President James A. Garfield was shot on July 2, 1881 and died on September 19, 1881. For nearly eighty days the executive branch was without a functioning head. Vice President Chester A. Arthur did not act as president during Garfield's incapacity. The Cabinet discussed the question and declined to declare Garfield unable: partly from constitutional uncertainty about who had the authority to make such a declaration, partly from fear of appearing to seize power, and partly from the Tyler-precedent trap — if Arthur became president, he could not step back down when Garfield recovered.

The result was a government that simply waited for a man to die or recover. No executive business requiring presidential action was transacted for the duration. Attorney General Wayne MacVeagh discussed the problem but issued no formal opinion on whether or how the Cabinet could declare inability. No one acted, because no one knew who was authorized to act, and because acting might be irreversible.

The Garfield episode is drawn from secondary historical sources (the Constitution Annotated analysis of pre-25th Amendment inability, PBS historical accounts) and has **not** been verified against primary documents to this project's page-image standard.

### Wilson, 1919–1920: the strongest case

President Woodrow Wilson suffered a massive stroke on October 2, 1919 and was substantially incapacitated for the remaining seventeen months of his term, which ended on March 4, 1921. This is the episode that most directly demonstrates the silence in operation, and four features of it matter.

**First, the concealment.** Wilson's physician, Dr. Cary Grayson, and the President's wife, Edith Wilson, controlled access to the President and concealed the severity of his condition from the Cabinet, Congress, and the public. Mrs. Wilson described her own role as a "stewardship": she screened all papers sent to the President, decided which matters were important enough to bring to his attention, and relayed his responses — or her interpretation of them — back to officials. For practical purposes the executive branch was being run by someone who held no constitutional office and had been elected to nothing.

**Second, the Cabinet's attempt and its failure.** Secretary of State Robert Lansing convened Cabinet meetings in Wilson's absence and raised the question of presidential inability. He was met with resistance from Dr. Grayson, who refused to certify that Wilson was unable to discharge his duties, and from Mrs. Wilson, who opposed any invocation of the inability clause. Lansing had no constitutional mechanism to override either of them, because no mechanism existed. In February 1920, Wilson demanded Lansing's resignation, on the stated ground that Lansing had convened Cabinet meetings without the President's authorization. The person the check was meant to constrain punished the person who tried to invoke it.

**Third, the Vice President's inaction.** Vice President Thomas Marshall stated that he would act only if Congress passed a concurrent resolution declaring the President unable, or if the Cabinet certified in writing that the President was unable to discharge his duties. Neither happened. Marshall did not act because he had no authority that anyone recognized, and he did not attempt to create authority because the Tyler precedent meant that acting might make him president permanently.

**Fourth, the duration.** Wilson's incapacity lasted seventeen months, from the stroke in October 1919 through the end of his term in March 1921. No inability was declared. No one acted as president. The government continued to function through the Cabinet's individual initiative and Mrs. Wilson's informal gatekeeping, but the presidency as a constitutional office was vacant in every respect except the name of the person who held it.

The Wilson episode is drawn from secondary historical sources (Constitution Annotated analysis, Edith Bolling Wilson Museum materials, general historical accounts) and has **not** been verified against primary documents (Wilson's papers, the Congressional Record for the relevant sessions, Lansing's own memoir) to this project's page-image standard.

### The Presidential Succession Act of 1947

The Presidential Succession Act was signed on July 18, 1947, inside the vantage, and `silence-inventory.md` row 12 already records it. What matters for this probe is what the Act does and does not say about inability.

The Act uses the word "inability" as a triggering condition: if "by reason of death, resignation, removal from office, inability, or failure to qualify, there is neither a President nor Vice President to discharge the powers and duties of the office," the Speaker of the House acts as President. And subsection (c)(2) provides that if the Speaker's discharge of the office "is founded in whole or in part on the inability of the President or Vice President, then he shall act only until the removal of the disability of one of such individuals."

The Act therefore acknowledges that inability can be temporary — the word "until" and the phrase "removal of the disability" presuppose recovery. But it does none of the following:

- It does not define "inability."
- It does not designate who determines that inability exists.
- It does not specify a procedure for declaring inability.
- It does not address the case where a President disputes that he is unable.
- It does not say who determines that the disability has been "removed."
- It applies only to the succession past the Vice President. It does not touch the primary devolution from President to Vice President, which is governed by the constitutional clause alone.

The route is `https://www.loc.gov/item/llsl-v61/?fo=json`, which names the scan at `https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/llsl/llsl-c80s1/llsl-c80s1.pdf`, SHA-256 `e4aa4d7c02333abf90c5055e50ba1b9eef40ef8685a06f9201733d4238e73ae8`, printed page 380 at PDF page 404, rendered with `pdftoppm -f 404 -l 404 -r 200 -png`. The retrieval problem `corpus/README.md` recorded for this volume is solved and the solution is written up there; the OCR layer in this file doubles every line and prints the date of the Act as 1917, which is the usual reason for reading the image instead.

Reading the page settled one thing the clause itself leaves open. The constitutional text says "Inability" twice and then "Disability" once, and whether those are the same condition is not obvious from the clause. Subsection (c)(2) of the 1947 Act uses both words for the same condition inside a single sentence — service founded on "the inability of the President or Vice President" ends at "the removal of the disability of one of such individuals." Congress, legislating in the vantage year against the very clause in question, treated them as interchangeable. That is not authority about what the framers meant, and it does not name a judge of the condition, but it is contemporaneous evidence on a question this file had recorded as unresolved, and it comes from a page image rather than an inference.

## Answers to the four questions

All answers are as of December 5, 1947.

### (a) Was there any authority determining who judges inability?

**No.** The constitutional text does not say. No statute said. No judicial decision said. No precedent established by practice said, because the clause had never been successfully invoked. Three episodes — 1881, 1919–1920, and the lesser cases of the several occasions on which presidents underwent surgery under anesthesia — had raised the question, and in every instance the answer was the same: nobody knew, so nobody acted.

The 1947 Succession Act uses the word "inability" as a trigger but does not say who pulls it. The constitutional clause authorizes Congress to legislate the line of succession past the Vice President, but that authorization, on its face, concerns declaring "what Officer shall then act" — not defining what inability means for the first devolution.

### (b) Was there any mechanism for a President to be declared unable against his will?

**No.** The Wilson episode is the direct demonstration. Lansing attempted to raise the question within the Cabinet. He had no procedure to follow, no threshold to meet, no body authorized to decide, and no way to override the President's physician or the President's wife, neither of whom held a constitutional office relevant to the question. The President then fired Lansing for trying.

A president who denied his own inability could not be overridden by anyone, because no one had been given the authority to override him. The clause says "the Same shall devolve" but does not say on whose judgment the devolution occurs.

### (c) Was there any mechanism for an inability to end?

**Almost nothing.** The constitutional clause says "until the Disability be removed, or a President shall be elected," and the 1947 Succession Act echoes "until the removal of the disability." Both presuppose that a disability can be removed, but neither says who decides that it has been. And the Tyler precedent made this question worse: if devolution means the Vice President becomes president permanently, there is nothing to end, because the original President is no longer President.

The only mechanism implicit in the text is the election of a new President at the next regular election, which is not a mechanism for ending inability but for outlasting it.

### (d) Does this constitute the self-judged-trigger failure mode?

**Yes, in a specific and demonstrable way, but with a qualification.**

`method/attack-surfaces.md` describes the self-judged-trigger failure as the case where "the condition that opens the power is assessed by the person who benefits from opening it." The inability clause is a candidate instance, but not in the obvious direction.

The obvious reading would be that the Vice President benefits from declaring the President unable and therefore should not be the judge. That reading is real but was never tested, because no vice president in the pre-1947 record ever attempted to invoke the clause.

The demonstrated failure runs the other way. A president who is unable benefits from *not* being declared unable, because the declaration (under the Tyler reading) would permanently cost him the office. And since no one else is authorized to make the judgment, the president's own denial of inability — or, as in Wilson's case, the denial issued by persons acting under the president's domestic authority rather than any constitutional mandate — is dispositive. **The trigger for the devolution is, in practice, self-judged by the person the devolution is meant to displace.** The president who should be checked is the one who decides whether the check fires.

The Wilson episode is the proof that this is not hypothetical. Wilson's inability was medically manifest, practically acknowledged by everyone who dealt with the executive branch, and politically consequential for seventeen months. The check did not fire because the person it was meant to constrain — through his wife and physician, neither of whom held relevant constitutional office — prevented it from firing, and no one had been given the authority to override that prevention.

The qualification is important: this is not a *hidden* defect. Everyone knew about it. It was discussed in Congress after Garfield, after Wilson, and during the debate over the 1947 Succession Act. The gap was common knowledge, not a discovery, and it was later closed by the Twenty-fifth Amendment in 1967 (which is outside the vantage and is noted here only to record that the question was eventually settled). A known defect that was openly discussed and eventually repaired by constitutional amendment is a different thing from a hidden structural flaw that follows the rules to an unintended destination.

## Against `method/what-counts-as-a-finding.md`

**This is not a finding.** It fails on three of the disqualifiers, and the determination is clear enough that no close judgment is needed.

**It fails novelty.** The inability gap was discussed in Congress and in the legal literature continuously from 1881 forward. It was raised during the 1947 Succession Act debate. It was a commonplace of constitutional law commentary in 1947. Nothing here was unavailable to any reader of the Congressional Record.

**It fails the "name the edit that closes it" test, but only trivially.** The edit that closes it is the Twenty-fifth Amendment, which was in fact adopted. That an amendment was needed and later supplied is evidence that this was a genuine constitutional deficiency. But a deficiency that everyone agreed on and that required an amendment to fix is not what this project means by a loophole. It is a known gap, not a hidden path.

**It fails the "requires only bad faith" test.** An incapacitated president whose household conceals the incapacity is not exploiting a structural flaw in the text. The text has a structural flaw — the missing designation of a judge — but what made it operative in the Wilson case was not the flaw alone but the willingness of specific people to conceal a medical fact. Any constitution that relies on human actors can be defeated by those actors lying. That is a fact about trust, not about the document.

**The self-judged-trigger match is real but does not save it.** The failure mode described in `attack-surfaces.md` is a structural feature of the clause, not a behavioural one, and this probe confirms it is present. But `what-counts-as-a-finding.md` requires a path — numbered steps, citations, a minimum coalition — and the inability silence does not yield one. There are no steps to enumerate because there is nothing to do: the silence operates by the absence of any procedure, and absence is not a sequence of legal operations. The "coalition" is one person (the President, or someone acting under the President's personal authority), which is impressively small, but the "path" is simply to deny being unable, and denying a fact is not a legal operation authorized by a clause.

## What this probe is worth

The inability silence is genuine, it was the worst gap in the 1947 constitutional order by the measure of having no filler at all, and it maps to the self-judged-trigger failure mode with a historical demonstration. It is a real defect in the text. It is not a finding under this project's standard because it was universally known and because it operates by omission rather than by a sequence of rule-following steps.

It is worth recording for two reasons. First, it closes the question of whether row 11 should be investigated further in a different direction: it should not, because the inability silence does not connect to Article V, does not touch ratification, and does not produce a path to dictatorship. It produces a period during which the presidency is effectively vacant or effectively controlled by unelected persons, which is bad but is not what Gödel claimed to have found. Second, the self-judged-trigger match is a clean instance of the failure mode, and having a documented instance against a real clause makes the category in `attack-surfaces.md` concrete rather than abstract.

## Source status

The two statutory texts in this file are verified. The constitutional clause was read from the NARA transcription in the corpus, and the Presidential Succession Act of 1947 was read from the Library of Congress page scan of 61 Stat. 380, quotation by quotation, as recorded above. **Every *historical* claim, by contrast, is drawn from secondary sources** — web-accessible historical accounts, the Constitution Annotated analysis hosted by Cornell Law Institute and Congress.gov, and general reference materials — **and none of those has been checked against primary documents (the Congressional Globe, the Congressional Record, or presidential papers) to this project's page-image standard.** The distinction matters: what the law said in 1947 is verified here, and what people did about it in 1881 and 1919 is not.

The specific claims that would most benefit from primary-source verification, in order of load-bearing importance, are:

1. That Wilson demanded Lansing's resignation for convening Cabinet meetings, and that Wilson's stated ground was the unauthorized nature of the meetings — this is the direct evidence for the self-judged-trigger match.
2. That Vice President Marshall conditioned his willingness to act on a written Cabinet certification or a concurrent resolution of Congress — this establishes that the gap was understood at the time to be a gap, not merely a matter of political caution.
3. That the gap was raised in Congress during the debate on the 1947 Act — the Act's own text is now verified, but the legislative history around it is not, and it is the legislative history that carries the novelty judgment below.
