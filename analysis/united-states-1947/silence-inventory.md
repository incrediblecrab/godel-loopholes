# Silence inventory, United States, December 5, 1947

The procedure is in `method/enumerations.md`. It lists the fields the constitution does not reserve to itself, where ordinary legislation governs and no constitutional provision constrains what that legislation may say. It terminates in fields where a bare legislative majority can alter the conditions of its own re-election or tenure.

Statute citations below are given as they are ordinarily reported and have **not** been verified against Statutes at Large. The `llsl` retrieval problem recorded in `corpus/README.md` is unsolved, so no volume after 1 is currently reachable. The two judicial decisions carrying weight here were read from Library of Congress page images and are marked accordingly.

## The inventory

| # | Clause | What the text does not fix | What filled it in 1947 | Who can change the filler |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Art. I §2 | Size of the House. The text sets only a ceiling of one per thirty thousand and a floor of one per state | 435 — but no statute says so. The Act of August 8, 1911 fixed the House at 433 (37 Stat. 13 §1) and added one each for Arizona and New Mexico on admission (37 Stat. 14 §2); the Act of 1929 perpetuates "the then existing number" without naming it (46 Stat. 21, §22 at 26) | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |
| 2 | Art. I §2 | Method of apportioning representatives among states | Equal proportions, by the Act of November 15, 1941 (55 Stat. 761), amending §22 of the 1929 act | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |
| 3 | Art. I §4 | Districting standards. Nothing requires single-member districts, contiguity, compactness, or population equality | State law. The Act of 1911 had required districts of "contiguous and compact territory" containing "as nearly as practicable an equal number of inhabitants" (37 Stat. 14 §3); the Act of 1929 carried none of it forward | State legislatures, or Congress overriding under the same clause |
| 4 | Art. I §5 | What limits the power of each House to judge the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own members | The practice of each House. No constitutional limit is stated, and *Powell v. McCormack* is twenty-two years away | Each House alone, bare majority, no presentment |
| 5 | Art. I §5 | The base of the quorum. "A Majority of each" does not say a majority of what | Practice: members chosen and sworn, which row 4 controls | Each House alone, through row 4 |
| 6 | Art. I §5 | What the Rules of Proceedings may contain | The standing rules of each House | Each House alone, bare majority, no presentment, no judicial review |
| 7 | Art. III §1 | Number of justices. The text says "one supreme Court" and gives no number | Nine, by the Act of April 10, 1869 (16 Stat. 44), which set "the Chief Justice of the United States and eight associate justices, any six of whom shall constitute a quorum" | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |
| 8 | Art. III §1 | Existence, structure and abolition of every court below the Supreme Court | The Judicial Code | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |
| 9 | Art. III §2 | The scope of the Exceptions Clause. No floor is stated below which appellate jurisdiction may not be cut | The Judicial Code. *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. 506 (1869), withdrew jurisdiction in a case already argued and the Court acquiesced | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |
| 10 | Art. II §1 | Manner of appointing presidential electors. Nothing requires a popular vote | State law; popular vote in all forty-eight. *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), confirms the legislature's power is plenary | State legislatures |
| 11 | Art. II §1 | What "Inability" means, and who determines that it exists | **Nothing.** No statute, no rule, no decision. The Twenty-fifth Amendment is twenty years away | Nobody. This is unfilled silence, not delegated silence |
| 12 | Art. II §1 | Who acts as President beyond the Vice President | The Presidential Succession Act of July 18, 1947 (61 Stat. 380), placing the Speaker of the House second | Congress, bare majority plus presentment |

Row 11 is the only entry where the silence has no filler at all. Rows 4, 5 and 6 are the only entries a single chamber can alter with no concurrence from anyone: no other chamber, no President, and on the 1947 law no court.

## The cluster, and who found it first

Rows 1 through 6 are not independent. Article V's congressional threshold is two thirds of the members present assuming a quorum, established in `threshold-arithmetic.md`. The quorum is a majority of the House. The House's membership is what row 4 lets the House itself determine. So the denominator of the Article V fraction is set by the body the fraction is meant to constrain, and rows 7 through 9 govern whether any of that is reviewable.

That is a provision reaching itself, which is what the reference graph in `method/enumerations.md` is meant to detect, and it was reached here by the silence inventory instead.

The cluster is not ours. *Colegrove v. Green*, 328 U.S. 549, decided June 10, 1946, eighteen months before the vantage date, is a districting challenge in which Justice Rutledge, concurring in the result, wrote that Article I §4, Article I §2 and Article I §5 "would remove the issues in this case from justiciable cognizance" but for *Smiley v. Holm*. He named rows 3, 1 and 4 as a group, a year and a half before Gödel's hearing. Read from the page image at https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep328/usrep328549/usrep328549.pdf.

*Colegrove* also has to be stated more carefully than it usually is. Frankfurter's nonjusticiability opinion, with its warning that courts "ought not to enter this political thicket," was joined by two justices, not four. Jackson took no part, three dissented, and Rutledge concurred only in the result while saying in terms that he thought the issues justiciable. The proposition that malapportionment was judicially unreachable in 1947 rested on a three-vote plurality plus a fourth vote that expressly disclaimed the reasoning.

## Disqualification

Against `method/what-counts-as-a-finding.md`, this is not a finding, and it fails on two counts.

It fails novelty. Row 9 is *McCardle* and has been argued over continuously since 1869. Row 7 is the 1937 Court-packing plan, which was six years in the past and politically fresh. Rows 1 through 4 were grouped by a sitting justice in 1946 in a published opinion. Nothing in the table was unavailable to any reader of the U.S. Reports.

It fails as a path. Every row is a route to controlling Congress and the federal courts. None of them touches ratification, and `threshold-arithmetic.md` establishes that ratification is where Article V's protection actually sits. Thirty-six of forty-eight state legislatures are required, and no clause in this table reaches them. Row 10 gives state legislatures plenary power over presidential electors, but that is the states holding power over the federal executive, not the reverse.

The one item worth carrying forward is the row 4 to row 5 linkage, that exclusion of members shrinks the base from which Article V's two thirds is computed. It has not been located in the literature. It is also arithmetically small, politically expensive, and reversible by the states refilling the seats, and it does not clear the ratification barrier either. It is recorded as a question, not a candidate.

## The statutes, read

The four statutes in rows 1, 2, 7 and 12 were asserted rather than verified when this inventory was first written, because the route to the Statutes at Large scans had not been worked out. It has been, and it is recorded in `corpus/README.md`. All four have now been read from Library of Congress page images, and so has a fifth the table had not cited at all.

Rows 2, 7 and 12 came back clean. The Act of November 15, 1941 does prescribe equal proportions and does amend §22 of the 1929 act. The Act of April 10, 1869 does set the Court at a Chief Justice and eight associates — and, in the same sentence, makes six of them a quorum, which is the same statutory-quorum pattern as rows 4 and 5 sitting in a branch this table had not looked at that way. The Presidential Succession Act does place the Speaker second, and `inability-probe.md` reads it more closely.

Row 1 did not come back clean, and the correction is worth more than the row. **No statute states that the House has 435 members.** The Act of August 8, 1911 fixed it at "four hundred and thirty-three Members" (37 Stat. 13, §1) and then provided that Arizona and New Mexico, on admission, "shall have one Representative each, which Representative or Representatives shall be in addition to the number four hundred and thirty-three" (37 Stat. 14, §2). Both were admitted in 1912, and 433 became 435 by that addition rather than by enactment. The Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 then perpetuated the figure without ever naming it: §22 speaks three times of apportioning "the then existing number of Representatives," and its marginal note records the default as each State keeping "its existing number." The Act of 1941 repeats the same phrase. So the number is inherited by reference, and has been since 1912.

That matters here for a reason beyond bookkeeping. `threshold-arithmetic.md` computes Article V's congressional thresholds from 435, and this table's first row is where that number is supposed to come from. It comes from a repealed-in-substance 1911 enactment of a different number, plus two admissions, carried forward by a phrase. The Constitution's silence about the size of the House is filled by a statute that is itself silent about it. Nothing in the arithmetic changes — 435 was the correct figure on December 5, 1947 — but the provenance is one link longer and one degree more contingent than the row implied, and a reader who went looking for "435" in the 1929 act would not find it.

Row 3 was strengthened rather than corrected. The claim that the 1929 act carried forward none of the earlier districting standards is now verified from both sides: the 1911 act did require districts of "contiguous and compact territory" containing "as nearly as practicable an equal number of inhabitants" (37 Stat. 14, §3), and the 1929 act contains no such language anywhere. Congress prescribed those standards, then withdrew them while keeping the power to prescribe them, which is what makes row 3 a live silence in 1947 rather than a dormant one.

## What this run still does not cover

The inventory is drawn from constitutional text alone, and the statute book behind the fourth column has now been checked only where the table already pointed. Statutes the table does not cite have not been searched, so a filler that exists and was missed would still be missed. The pre-1947 practice underlying rows 4, 5 and 6 is treated in `quorum-base.md`, which carries its own caveat about the source it had to use.

Row 6 is the largest gap. The standing rules of the House and Senate as they existed in 1947 are not in this corpus, and `corpus/README.md` already records that chamber rules of procedure are a missing document category. Austria is the reason that matters: the March 1933 self-elimination turned on a gap in the Nationalrat's rules of procedure, not in its constitution. Row 6 is the same category of document, and it is the row this project is currently least equipped to examine.
