# Power inventory, United States, December 5, 1947

The procedure is in `method/enumerations.md`. It walks the document clause by clause, and wherever a clause confers a power on anyone, records six things: who holds it, what condition opens it, what it reaches, who can stop it, what bounds it in time, and what happens to the stopper if the person holding the power is hostile to them. It terminates in rows where the sixth column is empty or self-referential.

The sixth column is the one that does the work. It exists because of the third failure mode in `method/attack-surfaces.md`: the operator can be disabled. Both interwar enabling instruments carried a parliamentary revocation check, and a table with five columns records both as checked. A table with six records that the check fires only if a legislature is sitting, and that whether it sits is not under the legislature's own control. What follows applies that question to the United States Constitution as it stood on 12/5/1947.

All constitutional citations are from `corpus/united-states-1947/09-17-1787-constitution-parchment-nara-transcription.txt` and `corpus/united-states-1947/02-07-1795-amendments-11-21-nara-transcription.txt`, both National Archives transcriptions set from primary documents. Judicial decisions referenced in other analysis files in this folder — *National Prohibition Cases* in `threshold-arithmetic.md`, Hinds' *Precedents* in `quorum-base.md` — were verified there from Library of Congress page images and are not re-verified here. *Ex parte McCardle* and *Luther v. Borden* are cited conventionally and have **not** been page-image-verified to this project's standard.

## The inventory

Approximately one hundred and seventy power-conferring clauses were walked across the seven articles and twenty-one amendments in force on the vantage date. Most are non-terminating: the stopper survives the holder's hostility. Six rows terminate. They are listed here; the non-terminating rows are not.

## Legal status of each row

**Row 1, settled that the power exists; contested as to scope.** The Senate rejected the Langer exclusion on 3/27/1942, five years before the vantage, with the winning argument being that qualifications beyond Article I could not be added by majority vote. The Roberts exclusion (House, 1900) and the Smith and Vare exclusions (Senate, 1928-1929) went the other way. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), which would settle the question, is twenty-two years past the vantage. In 1947 the scope of this power is genuinely unsettled. Sourcing note: the Langer account is from the Senate Historical Office's *Election, Expulsion and Censure Cases: 1793-1990*, a secondary source, and has **not** been checked against the Congressional Record. This caveat is recorded in `quorum-cascade-null.md`.

**Row 2, settled.** Each House's plenary authority over its rules is unquestioned and was so in 1947.

**Row 3, settled that the power exists; novel that the check is self-referential.** The two-thirds requirement is on the face of the text and nobody disputes the authority. That the check is structurally self-referential — the same body that exercises the power constitutes the check — is this project's classification, not a holding. No case has addressed it as a structural defect.

**Row 4, contested as to holder; settled as to the conditions; novel as to the combination of three failure modes.** Whether Congress or the President holds the suspension power was contested in 1861 (*Ex parte Merryman*, Taney C.J. sitting as circuit justice, arguing for Congress alone) and never resolved by a Supreme Court majority. Congress ratified Lincoln's suspensions retroactively in the Habeas Corpus Suspension Act of 3/3/1863. That the conditions of suspension are self-judged, the operator empty, and the duration unstated is this project's structural observation. Each failure mode individually is recognized in the literature; the simultaneous presence of all three in one clause has not been located there.

**Row 5, settled that Congress can strip specific jurisdictional routes; contested whether a constitutional floor exists.** *McCardle* (1869) is the settled holding. Whether Congress could strip all appellate jurisdiction, leaving no route to constitutional review, was not tested and remains contested in 1947. That the power is structurally self-referential — removing the body that would check the removal — is this project's framing, though the underlying concern is as old as *McCardle* itself. *McCardle* is **not** page-image-verified.

**Row 6, contested.** Whether Article V can amend its own entrenchment is the classical question in the self-amendment literature and the subject of the formal model reproduced in `formal-model-replication.md`. The two-step argument (remove the entrenchment, then amend what it protected) is well-known. The literature is divided on whether the entrenchment is itself amendable, and the question has never been adjudicated. The minimum coalition (thirty-six state legislatures) is a finding of `threshold-arithmetic.md`.

## The cluster, and its convergence with the silence inventory

Rows 1, 2 and 3 are not independent. `silence-inventory.md` rows 4, 5 and 6 reached the same three clauses from the other direction: what the text does not fix rather than what it empowers someone to do. The convergence is exact. Article I, Section 5 appears in both inventories because the same provisions simultaneously grant a power and leave it unchecked, which is a silence and a power at once.

The combined picture: each House judges who sits (row 1), which determines the quorum base (`quorum-base.md` confirms this is members chosen and sworn, not the statutory chamber size); the quorum base determines two thirds of a quorum, which is the Article V proposing threshold under *National Prohibition Cases*, 253 U.S. 350 (1920) (`threshold-arithmetic.md`); and each House's rules (row 2) govern how those votes are conducted. Expulsion (row 3) can shrink the sitting membership further. The denominator of Article V's congressional fraction is therefore set by the body that fraction is meant to constrain.

That cluster is not ours. Justice Rutledge named three of its components — Article I §4, §2 and §5 — as a group in his concurrence in *Colegrove v. Green*, 328 U.S. 549, decided 6/10/1946, eighteen months before the vantage date. The silence inventory records this with the citation. `quorum-cascade-null.md` examined whether the cluster produces a viable path and closed it: the minimum coalition needed to begin the cascade (a quorum, 267 members combined) strictly dominates the coalition needed to propose an amendment outright (179 members). The cascade is self-defeating on its own arithmetic.

The power inventory adds nothing the silence inventory and the cascade analysis did not already show. It reaches the same clauses, through a different enumeration, and confirms that each one terminates for the reason the silence inventory predicted: no external check, no external check, and a self-referential internal check.

## Important non-terminating rows

The following powers were examined and found non-terminating. The stopper survives the holder's hostility in each case.

**Art. I §4 cl. 1, congressional override of state election regulations.** The stopper is the presidential veto, which is a different actor and functions. Congress may override at two thirds, but that is the designed mechanism for all legislation, not a failure of the check. The self-referential nature of the subject matter — the body elected under these rules sets these rules — is a finding for the reference-graph enumeration in `method/enumerations.md`, not a sixth-column termination. Noted here because `quorum-cascade-null.md` identifies it as an unexamined alternative route.

**Art. II §2 cl. 1, Commander in Chief.** The stoppers are congressional appropriations (Article I §8 cl. 12 limits army appropriations to two years) and impeachment. If the President is hostile to Congress, Congress can refuse appropriations and can impeach. The Twentieth Amendment, Section 2, requires Congress to assemble at least once in every year, so the President cannot constitutionally prevent Congress from sitting. The stopper functions. The two-year appropriation window is a time bound, not a duration failure.

**Art. II §2 cl. 1, pardon power.** The stopper is the impeachment exception ("except in Cases of Impeachment") and Congress's impeachment power for abuse. The impeachment exception is explicit and structurally intact: a hostile President cannot pardon away an impeachment proceeding. However, the pardon can shield co-conspirators from criminal prosecution for actions supporting the President in exercising other powers, creating a cross-row effect that weakens enforcement of other checks without disabling those checks structurally. Whether the President may pardon himself is not addressed in the text and was entirely untested in 1947; if valid, the post-removal criminal prosecution contemplated by Article I §3 cl. 7 would be defeated, but the removal power itself would survive. Self-pardon is marked as **novel** and the stopper on the pardon power (impeachment) is assessed as surviving.

**Art. II §2 cl. 3, recess appointments.** The time bound functions: commissions expire at the end of the Senate's next session, and the Senate controls its own sessions under Article I §5.

**Art. II §3, presidential adjournment power.** The condition is "Disagreement between them, with Respect to the Time of Adjournment," which must be present for the power to activate. The stopper (agreement between the two Houses) is not in the President's control. The duration ("such Time as he shall think proper") is self-judged but can only be reached when the Houses have already failed to agree between themselves.

**Art. IV §4, Guarantee Clause.** This is a duty ("shall guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government") rather than an offensive power, with undefined scope and undefined remedies. *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849), held the question political and unreviewable, which makes the trigger self-judged by Congress. But the power's reach is so undefined that the row does not terminate in an answer; it terminates in an undefined scope, which is not the same thing. Noted rather than tabled.

## Disqualification

Against `method/what-counts-as-a-finding.md`, this inventory is not a finding, and it fails on two counts.

**It fails novelty.** Every terminating row names a power that was known, contested or litigated well before 12/5/1947. Row 1 was the subject of the Roberts, Smith, Vare and Langer proceedings. Row 4 was contested in *Merryman* (1861) and legislated around in 1863. Row 5 is *McCardle* (1869). Row 6 is the self-amendment question that has been in the literature since at least Douglas Linder's 1981 article, and earlier. The structural classification — "no check," "self-referential," "self-judged trigger" — is this project's, but the underlying observations are not.

**It fails as a path.** No row in this table, alone or in combination, reaches dictatorship. The Article I §5 cluster reaches the proposing stage of Article V but not ratification, and `quorum-cascade-null.md` closes even that route on arithmetic. The exceptions clause reaches judicial review but not the legislature or the executive. Habeas suspension reaches individual liberty but not the structure of government. Article V's self-amendment reaches everything but costs thirty-six state legislatures, which is Article V working as designed. None clears the minimum-coalition test at a number small enough to call a loophole.

The two items worth carrying forward are the convergence of this inventory with the silence inventory on the Article I §5 cluster, which is now confirmed from two independent enumerations, and the structural classification of habeas suspension as carrying three simultaneous check failures in one clause, which has not been located in the literature in that form.

## What this run did not cover

The inventory is drawn from constitutional text alone. Three gaps are material.

**Statutes in force.** A power whose check is statutory rather than constitutional — the Insurrection Act of 1807 delegating militia-calling authority to the President, the Presidential Succession Act of 7/18/1947 — would appear as a non-terminating row under a stopper this inventory cannot examine. Austria's enabling act of 7/24/1917 is the reason that matters: the power that dismantled the Austrian constitutional order was not in the constitution at all. An inventory confined to the constitutional text is blind to the Austrian class by construction, which is the third attack-surface class in `method/attack-surfaces.md`.

**Chamber rules.** Rows 2 and 3 terminate in the absence of external checks on each House's rules and proceedings, but the *content* of those rules as they stood in 1947 is not examined. The standing rules of the House and Senate are not in this corpus. `silence-inventory.md` row 6 identifies the same gap and notes the Austrian parallel: the March 1933 self-elimination of the Nationalrat turned on a gap in its rules of procedure, not in its constitution.

**Judicial decisions reading powers narrowly.** The sixth column asks what happens to the stopper if the holder is hostile, not what the holder has historically done. A power whose scope has been narrowed by construction — as the exclusion power was arguably narrowed by the Langer precedent in 1942 — would show a different termination profile if the narrowing were treated as binding. This inventory takes the text as written and notes the construction alongside it, which is the method's instruction, but a hostile actor who disregards non-binding precedent is not breaking a constitutional rule.
